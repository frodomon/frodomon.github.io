## Context

El Tablero de Otto began as a personal observation, not a brief. As an active member of the Magic: The Gathering community between 2012 and 2014, I kept hearing the same complaints from players living across traditional Lima neighborhoods (Pueblo Libre, San Miguel, Magdalena, Jesús María, San Borja, Miraflores, Surco, San Isidro): no store near home, available formats demanded high investment, the experience at the two dominant stores of the time was poor, and there was no comfortable space to play. At the same time, the generation that had discovered TCGs in the early 2000s was now economically independent and eager to play again. With a strategic partner who had a location facing PUCP, I decided to build the store that community was asking for — not as just another retail business, but as a service design exercise where, beyond designing the experience, I was the business.

## The Real Challenge

The business problem was never selling cards. It was giving someone a reason strong enough to leave home and stay three or four hours somewhere else — consistently, week after week. Any store can move product; very few get people to come back for reasons that have nothing to do with price.

## Four Kinds of Player, Three Customers

Instead of assuming "Magic players" were a single audience, I ran personal interviews and built archetypes around two variables that actually predicted behavior: risk aversion and disposable capital for the hobby. Four profiles emerged: **semi-professional** players (chasing international qualification, highly committed, almost never buying sealed product — only singles), **pseudo-competitive** players (showing up only when a prize was on the line), **amateur** players (playing for fun, no interest in competing), and **new** players (wanting to learn without a large upfront investment, building their collection slowly).

The decision that most defined the business wasn't who to add — it was who to leave out: I explicitly decided pseudo-competitive players weren't Otto's audience, because their behavior — showing up only for the prize, with no community commitment — undermined the play environment the other three segments relied on. The decision carried an immediate, measurable cost: these were players who filled prize events, and leaving them out meant giving up attendance and revenue already on the table. I took it because the asset holding the business together wasn't the turnout at any single tournament — it was the reason the other three segments came back every week.

## The Value Map: Explicit and Implicit

Before designing a single event, I mapped what players wanted across two layers. The explicit layer — what they said in interviews: parking, comfortable numbered tables, good ventilation, food and drinks without leaving the venue, good stock, good ambience, a rest zone. The implicit layer — what they didn't say directly but explained why they came back: belonging to a healthy community, kind treatment, finding expert players, feeling the space was special, the progression of "beating the strongest player," making friends, discovering new games. Designing only for the explicit layer builds a store. Designing for both builds a community that doesn't leave for a better price down the street.

## Designing the Customer Lifecycle

With archetypes and the value map in place, I designed a four-phase cycle meant to turn a casual player into an active brand promoter — and to have that promoter feed the acquisition of the next one:

**Phase 1 — Segment Acquisition:** each archetype had its own channel and tactic. I reached semi-professionals with competitive events offering international qualification. New players, with free workshops to learn the game and no-cost competitions, promoted through Facebook Ads. Amateurs, with casual formats free of competitive pressure.

**Phase 2 — Living the Otto Experience:** once on the floor, the goal was for players to experience the explicit values (the space, the service) and notice the implicit ones (belonging) without having them explained.

**Phase 3 — Otto Lover:** players who came back were brought into the brand's community groups — the first step in turning repeat attendance into a sense of belonging.

**Phase 4 — Otto Ambassador:** the cycle closed with events and incentives designed specifically for "Otto Lovers" to bring new friends — returning them to Phase 1, but through organic acquisition instead of paid. That return was the point: each turn of the cycle lowered the acquisition cost of the next one.

The cycle ran on concrete formats: two Sealed leagues per season, one Draft league per season, Pauper format and FNM (Friday Night Magic) running in parallel, each serving a different archetype.

## Community as Infrastructure

The physical store was only the entry point. Real retention lived in the community infrastructure built around it: WhatsApp groups segmented by game format (Commander, Pauper, Modern, Pokémon TCG, and even a dedicated "Otto - Quarantine" group during the pandemic), plus a Facebook community for card trading. Each group was, in practice, a channel dedicated to a distinct archetype — the same segmentation logic from Phase 1, sustained over time. One emergent use we never designed for: the groups started being used to coordinate where to eat after events — the clearest signal that the community had become self-sustaining.

## Running the Whole Business

Unlike a design project commissioned by a client, at Otto there was no line between "my deliverable" and "the business." Beyond service and brand design, I was responsible for supplier relationships, product import, accounting, legal business registration, web development and customer service protocols.

Designing the service while carrying the margin changes the decisions you make. A free workshop for new players wasn't an experience idea — it was a cost line I had to justify against that week's inventory. The low-cost formats for PUCP students came from knowing exactly how much I could give up per transaction and still cover rent. Every experience decision had a number behind it, and the number was mine.

The brand was part of the same package. Otto isn't a logo: it's a character with its own voice, written so the store would feel like a place rather than a point of sale.

## Expansion: When Pokémon Arrived

When we decided to bring in Pokémon TCG, I reused the same archetype segmentation process. A profile emerged that didn't exist in Magic: the **collector**, a customer who doesn't buy cards to play but special product (figures, poké balls). The framework held and absorbed that fifth profile without needing to be rebuilt. The experience built with Magic accelerated everything: only then did we start building e-commerce and catalog sales through WhatsApp Business.

## Resilience: The Pandemic and What's Next

The pandemic closed the store and pushed the hobby onto digital platforms — the "Otto - Quarantine" group documents that moment. With the store shut, a team of players from the community entered Pokémon's Team Challenge, the official online circuit, under the Otto name. They reached the final and placed second. Pokémon shipped the prizes to Lima.

The roadmap that came out of it includes relaunching the board games line, adapting tournaments to online formats (League of Legends, Legends of Runeterra), expanding Pokémon product lines, and finishing the e-commerce build — evidence that the business kept thinking strategically even under pressure, instead of just reacting.

## Impact

- El Tablero de Otto became one of Lima's leading TCG stores — validated by community demand, not marketing spend
- Sustained organic attendance growth driven entirely by peer referral, across all four archetype segments
- Four parallel event tracks (Sealed, Draft, Pauper, FNM), each with distinct format, pricing and prize structure
- Network expansion through partnerships with other Lima TCG stores (Ponte Mosca, Magic Place Cantuarias, HeroStore) that joined the mission to grow the player community
- A team formed within Otto's community reached the final of Pokémon's Team Challenge, the official online circuit — second place, mid-pandemic
- The archetype framework, originally mapped for Magic, was successfully reused when expanding into Pokémon TCG — validating it as a system, not a one-off solution
- Established the frameworks later applied at institutional scale: archetype modeling, service blueprint design, community-as-product strategy and iterative event design


## The Framework

> What Otto taught me, and what I still apply: designing for who you won't serve matters as much as designing for who you will. Loyalty isn't bought with price — it's built by giving people a shared identity. And a segmentation framework only proves it's real when it survives contact with a product or context that didn't exist when you designed it.
